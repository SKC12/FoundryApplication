Storm King's Thunder: A Review

Storm King's Thunder is quite dear to me. It was the first published Dungeons & Dragons adventure I ran as a DM. It was also part of the first and only campaign our group played all the way to level 20. And yet, if another DM would ask if I recommended it, I'm not sure what the answer would be. In this article I'll discuss my own experience running it, issues I encountered, and a couple of memorable moments.

The campaign deals with the aftermath of the breaking of the Ordning, the decree which defines the Giant society and its caste system. Lacking its constraints, the giants descend into infighting with severe consequences to small folk that now live in the land they used to rule. As players, your job is to first withstand surprise attacks from the various giant species, investigate what caused such chaos and attempt to resolve the conflict.

The campaign offers different starting points and choices that impact which sections of the book will be seen or entirely skipped. This format often requires extra work from the DM and may result in the players missing content that they would appreciate, but it also makes the campaign very malleable. A DM that enjoys adding his own spin on published material will have an easy time expanding it, and it offers a lot of material that can be partially used in a homebrew game.

Chapter 1: A Great Upheavel

The campaign starts with an introductory chapter that takes the group from level 1 to level 5. It can be safely skipped, the book offers a different starting point at chapter 2 for groups that already played another published adventure for that level range, such as Lost Mine of Phandelver.

Since our group was midway into a homebrew campaign, that was the option we took and as such I cannot offer my own experience with it. It seems a good enough starting point, but groups that choose to start at chapter 2 will not be missing anything essential for the campaign.

Chapter 2: Rumblings

Chapter 2 offers three starting points, the towns of Bryn Shander, Goldenfields and Triboar. All three are under attack by giants, and the party, along with some NPCs under control of the players, are tasked with defending the town and repelling the attack. The idea of giving the players an extra character to control was an interesting choice to make what would usually be an overwhelming fight into something more manageable, and it also allows the DM to inflict some casualties without endangering the group, signaling the dangers posed by the enemies.

Our choice of town was Triboar, under attack by a group of Fire Giants, and the dynamic worked very well. Adding extra characters can always run the risk of making a fight too complicated or just take too much time, but the NPCs were simple enough, and the party still at a level where the complexity was manageable.

After thwarting the threat, the group receives a few side-quests that lead them to the open world section of the campaign that is the third chapter.

Chapter 3: The Savage Frontier

Perhaps the most contentious chapter in the book, chapter 3 is a compilation of small events that can feel a bit too similar to random encounters. Its goal is for the players to travel all around the Savage Frontier and get to know its people, feel a bit more of the troubles caused by the giants, which it does a decent job at, but just running it as is can feel a bit disjointed. It works best when the DM can create a logical path for the players to follow, which requires some extra work. The scenarios themselves can also feel a bit too barebone, also needing a bit of DM magic.

For our group, these issues were not a problem. As we were already part of a homebrew campaign, this chapter allowed me to go back and develop a bit more of the original plot. I also enjoyed expanding the contents of the book when I felt it was needed, but it is easy to see how someone that just wants a campaign they can play out of the box may feel frustrated with its contents.

The transition to the next chapter occurs when the players meet a friendly giant that works as a guide to their next destination, an oracle that the group hopes will finally offer an answer to what is happening.

Chapter 4: The Chosen Path

Chapter 4 takes place mostly inside the temple called the Eye of the All-Father, where the group hopes to find the oracle. It is a pretty standard dungeon, with combat encounters, traps, and puzzles. While the temple doesn't really stand out as a great dungeon, it did lead to one of the most memorable moments in our group's gaming history.

In a certain section, there's a puzzle that must be solved by a giant touching a certain rune. If anyone else does it, bad things will happen. Luckly, WotC was nice enough to put the truly nasty runes away from the ground, out of reach of regular sized humanoids. Unfortunately, a particularly curious member of our group had a pair of Slippers of Spider Climbing and decided to start investigating from the very top. The end result was one party member instantly dead and all other on death saves. The only thing that prevented a greater tragedy was that the giant was still with them so he could feed the victims a couple of healing potions.

To this day we still remember fondly of that moment. I don't think it was a very good puzzle, it could have been a tragedy, and other groups may have hated a result like that. But that didn't really matter, it provided the opportunity for the chaos of DnD to do its magic and I don't think “Meteor Button” will ever stop being our own inside joke.

Eventually the group did get past the puzzle, found the oracle and got the lead that would take them to the strongest section of the whole book, the heist at the giant lair.

Chapter 8: Forge of the Fire Giants

Chapters 5 through 9 are the various giant lairs, but a group only needs to visit a single one. Our group ended up with Chapter 8, the lair of the fire giants. It is a bit of a shame that some of the best material in the game will just not be seen at all by the players, so it is a common advice to adapt this section and make it so the group has to visit multiple, if not all, giant lairs. I did not think that was needed, one big dungeon was enough for my group, but they really are the highlight of the book.

The enemies were dangerous enough that the group could just go in guns blazing, and was forced to rush straight for the main objective, pick up the prize and rush out, holding the duke's children as hostage. Not the most honorable method, but it was all for the greater good.

Chapter 10: Hold of the Storm Giants

After dealing with whichever dungeon the party ends up at in the previous step, the next course of action is always the lair of the Storm Giants.

This chapter is all about the internal politics of the giants. The action slows down a lot and the players can take advantage of their social skills to progress the plot. It's a welcome change of pace and resting point before the group starts rushing towards the endgame.

In the end, the group eventually finds out that the reason for all the chaos is the disappearance of the Storm Giant king, and that the enemy behind it all is a dragon, ancient enemies of the giants. So, to attempt to fix it all, they need to find the king.

Chapter 11: Caught in the Tentacles

Chapter 11 deals with the search for the king and its eventual rescue. Depending on how things go, the group may have to fight a Kraken at the very end. Fortunately, or unfortunately depending on your point of view, my group ended up leaving before its appearance and the chapter ended up fairly forgettable.

Chapter 12: Doom of the Desert

The final battle, and one of the coolest visuals in the campaign, this chapter unfortunately ended in quite a disappointing fashion for our group.

It all sounds so nice; the party, buffed to giant sized, accompanied by a group of Storm Giants, rushes towards the lair of an Ancient Blue Dragon. Unfortunately, the game mechanics did not agree with the narrative, and when that happens, the mechanics usually win.

Storm Giants are immune to lightning, which makes them a very lopsided opponent for a Blue Dragon, even an ancient one. It also means the Blue Dragon, if played smartly, will focus on the members of the group that are not immune to most of its damage, the party members. The result was that the players ended up playing extremely scared to avoid a deathly breath weapon while the giants pummeled the dragon. Not the ideal ending.

It is probably something I should have foreseen, and made sure the Storm Giants did not reach the Dragon as healthy or with full numbers, and maybe adapted the potions that were intended to make them strong enough to fight an Ancient Blue Dragon at level 11. There are always ways to fix issues like this one, but it is yet another layer of work for the DM.

Conclusion

Despite its disappointing ending, I did enjoy Storm King's Thunder quite a lot. It has glaring issues and requires a more work from the DM than the average campaign, but for someone that likes to pick a campaign apart and tinker with it, it offers a very solid baseline.
